Screening outcomes and dataset

We added 69 new effect sizes from 29 studies, spanning 15 species. Notably, 13 of the new studies (45%) were published in or before 2019, inside the search window of the original meta-analyses. Two of those thirteen were already in the dataset of Davies et al. (2020) — recovered independently by our search and re-extracted from the primary reports — leaving eleven (38%) that fell inside that window yet appeared in neither original synthesis. Six (20.7%) of the new studies were unpublished theses, reflecting the value of the multilingual and grey-literature search. Two model genera dominate the evidence: Drosophila (11 studies) and Poecilia (8 studies) together contribute 46 effect sizes (66%). When pooled with the two original datasets, the combined sample covers 33 species.

Because the two source datasets overlap in the primary studies they cover, the 40 studies carried over from Jones & DuVal (2019), the 58 from Davies et al. (2020) and the 29 added here do not sum to a count of unique studies. Resolving all three sources to distinct publications gives 94 unique primary studies: 30 are common to the two source datasets, two of our new studies were already in Davies et al., and one Jones & DuVal study code duplicates another in the same dataset. The extraction-validation chapter sets out the full study-level crosswalk and every accepted and rejected match.

Overall evidence for mate-choice copying

The pooled effect of social information stayed positive and significant on both scales in the combined data, but was appreciably smaller than the original estimates. Considered on its own, the new evidence was roughly half the original magnitude, with confidence intervals spanning the null.

Heterogeneity

Total heterogeneity was high in every model (\(I^2 > 74\%\)). It accumulated mainly within studies (residual, effect-size level) and among species, not between studies, which contributed little (< 8%). Phylogenetic signal was negligible (\(I^2 < 7\%\)).

Taxonomic group

All group means were positive on both scales. On the Hedges’ g scale the point estimates rose from arthropods to fish to other vertebrates, and the among-group test only approached significance; on the log-odds scale the ranking differed and the groups did not differ significantly.

Exploratory (non-pre-registered) moderators

Beyond taxonomic group we examined copying mechanism, cue modality, observer mating status, and demonstrator age. These were exploratory, several levels are sparsely sampled, and no moderator explained a detectable share of the heterogeneity (all Wald \(Q_M\), p > 0.2; total \(I^2\) stayed above 80%). Two contrasts are worth noting.

Show R code 
mods <- data.frame(
  Contrast = c("Copying mechanism (combined, Hedges' g)",
               "Copying mechanism (new, Hedges' g)",

                 "generalized 0.26 vs individual 0.16",

                 "virgin 1.75 vs non-virgin 1.91"),

Generalized and individual copying were statistically indistinguishable (if anything, generalized was marginally stronger). Unlike Jones & DuVal (2019), the new extraction did not reproduce stronger copying among virgin observers — it tended to reverse it — and the difference vanished once pooled with the original binary data. Full model summaries and orchard plots for every moderator are in the Overall Effect and Moderator chapters (see sidebar).

Small-study effects, time-lag, and sensitivity

We found strong small-study effects on both scales and a time-lag decline only for the binary outcomes. Under the more conservative correction the adjusted mean approached zero — and for Hedges’ g fell significantly below it — whereas the bias-robust estimator kept a positive but reduced effect.

Robustness: leave-one-out and contentious effect sizes

The pooled estimates were robust. In leave-one-study-out and leave-one-species-out analyses every interval overlapped the full-sample estimate; the largest shift in any pooled mean was ≤ 0.07 units (0.02 g / 0.04 log-OR leaving out studies; 0.04 g / 0.07 log-OR leaving out species). Removing Poecilia reticulata — the strongest-copying, most-sampled species — nudged the mean toward zero but changed neither sign nor significance.

Bottom line

The qualitative conclusion of both original syntheses survives an expanded, more rigorous update: animals do copy the mate choices of others, and the pooled effect is positive and significant on both scales. But the effect is more modest and more context-dependent than previously implied — roughly half the original magnitude on its own, surrounded by large heterogeneity, and drawn close to (or below) zero under the more conservative bias correction.
