What this document validates

We are updating a mate-choice-copying meta-analysis built on two existing datasets that overlap in the primary studies they cover but report different effect-size metrics:

  • Davies et al. (2020) — Hedges’ d
  • Jones & DuVal (2019) — log odds ratio (lnOR)

Both are standardized effect sizes: different numerical scales for the same underlying quantity — how strongly an observer’s mate preference shifts toward the male it saw being chosen — and one scale can be converted into the other.

Before pooling them we need to know: for the studies both teams extracted, do the effect sizes agree once placed on a common scale? This is a method-comparison problem (two “instruments” measuring the same underlying effects), so — unlike a single-team re-extraction audit — we can apply formal agreement statistics (correlation, Bland–Altman limits of agreement) on top of the per-study qualitative review.

The matching itself (study-level crosswalk + within-study optimal effect-size assignment) is performed by crosscheck_davies_jones.py; this report reads its output and assesses agreement. Key methodological points carried over:

  • Scale conversion. Jones lnOR is mapped to Hedges’ d via the logistic-to-normal approximation \(d = \text{lnOR}\times\sqrt{3}/\pi\) (≈ 0.5513; Borenstein et al. 2009).
  • Absolute magnitudes. The two datasets use opposite sign conventions (Davies’ non-directional d carries the raw mean-difference sign; Jones’ lnOR sign encodes copying direction), so agreement is assessed on \(|d|\). Sign concordance is reported separately as a diagnostic.

How this document is organised. The analysis runs in five steps: (1) match studies — find the studies both datasets extracted and pair up their individual effect sizes; (2) measure agreement — quantify how closely the paired values agree once on a common scale; (3) classify disagreements — for pairs that do not agree, label why; (4) re-extract the worst cases — return to the original PDFs and recompute the effect size from the raw data; and (5) conclude. Throughout, “Jones” and “Davies” name the two source datasets, not the primary studies they drew from. A pair is a single Jones effect size lined up with the one Davies effect size that most plausibly measures the same thing in the same study (the pairing is done automatically, choosing the alignment that minimises the magnitude difference within each study).

Method-comparison agreement statistics

With the effect sizes paired, we ask how well the two datasets agree as measurements of the same thing — the classic question of whether two instruments give the same reading. Three complementary tools answer it:

  • Correlation (Pearson r, Spearman ρ) — do the two rise and fall together? (1 = perfectly in step.)
  • Regression slope of Davies on Jones — is one dataset systematically larger than the other? (a slope of 1 = no scaling difference.)
  • Bland–Altman — for each pair, the difference between the two values plotted against their average; the mean difference is the typical bias, and the 95% “limits of agreement” bound how far apart the two datasets usually fall.

Discrepancy classification

Borrowing the per-study “required action” structure from Sánchez-Tójar et al.’s validation codebook, every pair is classified by why it does or does not agree. The dominant structural cause is whether the two teams extracted the same number of effect sizes from a paper.

The three causes mean very different things. Agree needs no action. ES-count / extraction structure differs means the two teams split a study into different numbers of effect sizes, so a clean one-to-one comparison is impossible — a bookkeeping difference, not necessarily an error. Same structure, magnitude conflict is the category that matters: the teams extracted the same number of effect sizes yet still disagree, which signals a genuine extraction difference. Re-extraction: what happens when we return to the source PDFs

The agreement analysis flags where the two datasets disagree, but not which is correct. To resolve that for the worst cases, we pulled the primary-study PDFs for every study whose effect-size pair disagreed by > 0.5 Hedges-d units and re-extracted each effect size from the raw data, recomputing it as a 2×2 odds ratio — tabulating how many observers chose the demonstrated male versus the alternative, in the treatment and (where one exists) in the control, and taking the odds of one over the other — under the meta-analysis’s own conventions (treatment-vs-control where the study used a control group; the observed proportion against 50% chance otherwise). This gives a third, independent estimate per effect size — a tie-breaker.

Across the 12 re-extracted effect sizes the point estimates sit almost exactly on the Jones values (mean |Δd| = 0.00) and far from the Davies values (mean |Δd| = 0.83). Two cautions stop this from being read as “the extraction is validated against Jones”:

  • Point-estimate agreement is expected, not corroborating. These outcomes are discrete choice counts read directly from each PDF; any careful extractor building the same 2×2 obtains the same lnOR. Agreement here confirms we read the papers the same way — it is not independent evidence that either prior extraction is correct.
  • The original crosswalk selected the construction that matched Jones. For each study it chose the 2×2 that reproduced Jones’s variance, which makes the variance match circular. The next section removes that circularity: it re-derives every effect size from a rule fixed by the study design before any comparison to Jones, and reports count agreement and design agreement separately.

Count agreement vs design agreement (and a correction to Jones)

To break the circularity we re-derive each effect size from a rule fixed by the study design alone — before looking at Jones — and report two things separately: count agreement (does our lnOR from the raw counts match Jones, i.e. did we read the paper the same way) and design agreement (does our variance and N match, i.e. did we model the design the same way). The rule has two branches:

  • Real control group — the 2×2 has distinct treatment and control cells (e.g. Dugatkin 1992’s naïve non-observer group). A standard 2×2 odds ratio is valid: \(\mathrm{Var}=1/A+1/B+1/C+1/D\), \(N=A+B+C+D\).
  • Single group vs 50 % chance — the “50/50 rule” sets \(C=D=n/2\). The design-faithful estimator is the one-sample logit of the observed proportion: \(\mathrm{Var}=1/A+1/B\), \(N=A+B\). The 50/50 group is a phantom control: it invents \(n\) observations that were never collected and inflates the variance by exactly \(4/n\) while doubling the reported \(N\).
Show R code 
reext2 <- reext %>%

Why Davies diverges — four failure modes

The re-extraction shows the discrepancies are not random noise or conversion error; they trace to four identifiable problems, all on the Davies side:

  1. Different outcome construct. For Applebaum & Cruz 2000 and Briggs et al. 1996, Davies extracted a secondary affiliation-time measure (often non-significant) rather than the preference-reversal frequency the studies analyse; for Howard et al. 1998 Davies extracted a male-size preference — a different question entirely.
  2. Computational artifacts. Dugatkin 1992’s Davies d = 3.732 is exactly 2 + √3, the signature of a degenerate-2×2 escalc input; Gierszewski et al. 2018’s d = 2.43 is inflated by the structural zero cell at first choice.
  3. Sign error. Howard et al. 1998: Davies records +1.14 for a study that found no copying (females retained their original preference 15:5); the re-extraction and Jones both give −0.61.
  4. Pool-vs-split, not an error. Fowler-Finn et al. 2015 is the one case where neither is wrong: Davies pooled one effect (d 0.63) where Jones — and our re-extraction — split it into a copying arm (d 1.21) and a no-copying arm (d 0.10), matching the study’s significant phenotype × age interaction.

A design subtlety surfaced during re-extraction: for Dugatkin 1992 the copying tests are correctly compared against the study’s naïve control group, not against chance. Under that (correct) design Davies’s total N = 40 is right — only the magnitude (the 2 + √3 artifact) is wrong — while Jones’s third recorded value for this study (lnOR 2.234) is spurious: it reproduces from no pair of the paper’s six experiments and should be dropped.

How many unique primary studies are there?

The PRISMA flowchart counts 40 studies carried over from Jones & DuVal (2019), 58 from Davies et al. (2020) and 29 from our own extraction. Those three numbers do not sum to a count of unique primary studies, because the sources overlap, which is worth making explicit. This section resolves the corpus to distinct primary studies.

A study here is one primary publication contributing at least one usable effect size. Three things have to be settled to count them:

  1. Matches across sources. No two sources share an identifier, so candidates are generated from metadata and then adjudicated.
  2. Duplicates within a source. One source can carry the same primary study under two identifiers.
  3. Records that look like matches but are not — same author, adjacent year, same species, different paper.

Candidate generation, then explicit adjudication

Candidates are generated deliberately generously: same first-author surname prefix (three letters, accent-folded) and publication year within ±1. Species is reported alongside but never used to filter, so no potential match is silently discarded. Every candidate the data produce is then resolved by hand — accepted or rejected, with the reason recorded — against the source records (authors, year, species, sample size, outcome construct) and, where the two source teams disagreed, against the primary PDFs already consulted for the re-extraction above.

The adjudication is an explicit list rather than a threshold rule, and the code fails loudly if the data ever change such that a candidate is left unresolved, or a hand-coded decision no longer corresponds to a real candidate. That way the count cannot drift silently.

## What this document validates

We are updating a mate-choice-copying meta-analysis built on **two existing
datasets that overlap in the primary studies they cover but report different
effect-size metrics**:

* **Davies et al. (2020)** — Hedges' *d*
* **Jones & DuVal (2019)** — log odds ratio (lnOR)

Both are *standardized effect sizes*: different numerical scales for the same
underlying quantity — how strongly an observer's mate preference shifts toward
the male it saw being chosen — and one scale can be converted into the other.

Before pooling them we need to know: *for the studies both teams extracted, do
the effect sizes agree once placed on a common scale?* This is a
**method-comparison** problem (two "instruments" measuring the same underlying
effects), so — unlike a single-team re-extraction audit — we can apply formal
agreement statistics (correlation, Bland–Altman limits of agreement) on top of
the per-study qualitative review.

The matching itself (study-level crosswalk + within-study optimal effect-size
assignment) is performed by
[`crosscheck_davies_jones.py`](crosscheck_davies_jones.py); this report reads
its output and assesses agreement. Key methodological points carried over:

* **Scale conversion.** Jones lnOR is mapped to Hedges' *d* via the
  logistic-to-normal approximation $d = \text{lnOR}\times\sqrt{3}/\pi$
  (≈ 0.5513; Borenstein et al. 2009).
* **Absolute magnitudes.** The two datasets use opposite sign conventions
  (Davies' non-directional *d* carries the raw mean-difference sign; Jones'
  lnOR sign encodes copying direction), so agreement is assessed on $|d|$.
  Sign concordance is reported separately as a diagnostic.

**How this document is organised.** The analysis runs in five steps: (1) *match
studies* — find the studies both datasets extracted and pair up their individual
effect sizes; (2) *measure agreement* — quantify how closely the paired values
agree once on a common scale; (3) *classify disagreements* — for pairs that do
not agree, label *why*; (4) *re-extract the worst cases* — return to the original
PDFs and recompute the effect size from the raw data; and (5) *conclude*.
Throughout, "Jones" and "Davies" name the two source **datasets**, not the
primary studies they drew from. A *pair* is a single Jones effect size lined up
with the one Davies effect size that most plausibly measures the same thing in
the same study (the pairing is done automatically, choosing the alignment that
minimises the magnitude difference within each study).

## Method-comparison agreement statistics

With the effect sizes paired, we ask how well the two datasets agree *as
measurements of the same thing* — the classic question of whether two instruments
give the same reading. Three complementary tools answer it:

* **Correlation** (Pearson *r*, Spearman *ρ*) — do the two rise and fall together?
  (1 = perfectly in step.)
* **Regression slope** of Davies on Jones — is one dataset systematically larger
  than the other? (a slope of 1 = no scaling difference.)
* **Bland–Altman** — for each pair, the *difference* between the two values plotted
  against their *average*; the mean difference is the typical bias, and the 95%
  "limits of agreement" bound how far apart the two datasets usually fall.

## Re-extraction: what happens when we return to the source PDFs {#sec-reextraction}

The agreement analysis flags *where* the two datasets disagree, but not *which*
is correct. To resolve that for the worst cases, we pulled the primary-study
PDFs for every study whose effect-size pair disagreed by **> 0.5 Hedges-*d*
units** and re-extracted each effect size from the raw data, recomputing it as a
2×2 odds ratio — tabulating how many observers chose the demonstrated male
versus the alternative, in the treatment and (where one exists) in the control,
and taking the odds of one over the other — under the meta-analysis's own
conventions (treatment-vs-control where the study used a control group; the
observed proportion against 50% chance otherwise). This gives a third,
independent estimate per effect size — a tie-breaker.


being read as "the extraction is validated against Jones":

* **Point-estimate agreement is expected, not corroborating.** These outcomes are
  discrete choice counts read directly from each PDF; any careful extractor
  building the same 2×2 obtains the same lnOR. Agreement here confirms we *read the
  papers the same way* — it is not independent evidence that either prior
  extraction is correct.
* **The original crosswalk selected the construction that matched Jones.** For each
  study it chose the 2×2 that reproduced Jones's variance, which makes the variance
  match circular. The next section removes that circularity: it re-derives every
  effect size from a rule fixed by the study design **before** any comparison to
  Jones, and reports count agreement and design agreement separately.

### Count agreement vs design agreement (and a correction to Jones)

To break the circularity we re-derive each effect size from a rule fixed by the
study design alone — *before* looking at Jones — and report two things separately:
**count agreement** (does our lnOR from the raw counts match Jones, i.e. did we
read the paper the same way) and **design agreement** (does our variance and N
match, i.e. did we model the design the same way). The rule has two branches:

* **Real control group** — the 2×2 has distinct treatment and control cells (e.g.
  *Dugatkin 1992*'s naïve non-observer group). A standard 2×2 odds ratio is valid:
  $\mathrm{Var}=1/A+1/B+1/C+1/D$, $N=A+B+C+D$.
* **Single group vs 50 % chance** — the "50/50 rule" sets $C=D=n/2$. The
  design-faithful estimator is the one-sample logit of the observed proportion:
  $\mathrm{Var}=1/A+1/B$, $N=A+B$. The 50/50 group is a *phantom* control: it
  invents $n$ observations that were never collected and inflates the variance by
  **exactly $4/n$** while doubling the reported $N$.

```{r}
#| label: design-rederivation
reext2 <- reext %>%
    mutate(
        A = effectSizeSampleSizeA, B = effectSizeSampleSizeB,
        C = effectSizeSampleSizeC, D = effectSizeSampleSizeD,
        design = if_else(C == D, "single group vs 50% chance", "real control group"),
        corr_var = if_else(design == "real control group",
                           1/A + 1/B + 1/C + 1/D, 1/A + 1/B),
        corr_N   = if_else(design == "real control group", A + B + C + D, A + B),
        jones_var    = compare_jones_VlnOR,
        count_agree  = abs(reext_d - jones_d) < 0.02,   # point estimate (lnOR × √3/π)
        design_agree = abs(corr_var - jones_var) < 0.01
    )

reext2 %>% summarise(
    `Effect sizes` = n(),
    `Count agreement (lnOR matches Jones)`      = sum(count_agree),
    `Design agreement (variance matches Jones)` = sum(design_agree)
) %>% pivot_longer(everything(), names_to = "Quantity", values_to = "n") %>%
    kable()
```

Count agreement is complete; design agreement holds **only** for the real-control
studies, where a 2×2 is the correct model. The single-group studies disagree by
construction — and the disagreement is Jones's $4/n$ phantom-control inflation:

```{r}
#| label: phantom-control
reext2 %>%
    filter(design == "single group vs 50% chance") %>%
    transmute(Study = label,
              `our Var (1/A+1/B)` = round(corr_var, 4),
              `+ 4/n`             = round(4/(A + B), 4),
              `our Var + 4/n`     = round(corr_var + 4/(A + B), 4),
              `Jones Var`         = round(jones_var, 4),
              `our N` = corr_N,
              Note = if_else(abs(corr_var + 4/(A + B) - jones_var) < 0.01,
                             "Jones inflated by phantom 50/50 control",
                             "Jones used a different null")) %>%
    kable()
```

For every single-group study except *Howard 1998*, `our Var + 4/n` reproduces
Jones's variance to the fourth decimal, confirming the inflation is the invented
control group rather than a different reading. *Howard 1998* is the exception
(Jones used a different null), and our estimate is preferred there. So on the
vs-chance studies the design-faithful re-derivation **corrects** Jones — smaller
variance, halved $N$ — rather than reproducing him: the earlier "matches Jones
exactly" was, for these studies, matching a 50/50-rule artifact. The corrected
$N$ also reframes Davies's $N$ errors below: Davies's $N=40$ for *Dugatkin 1993*
is wrong against the design-faithful $N=20$, not merely against Jones.

### Why Davies diverges — four failure modes

The re-extraction shows the discrepancies are not random noise or conversion
error; they trace to four identifiable problems, all on the Davies side:

1. **Different outcome construct.** For *Applebaum & Cruz 2000* and *Briggs et al.
   1996*, Davies extracted a secondary **affiliation-time** measure (often
   non-significant) rather than the **preference-reversal frequency** the studies
   analyse; for *Howard et al. 1998* Davies extracted a **male-size preference** —
   a different question entirely.
2. **Computational artifacts.** *Dugatkin 1992*'s Davies *d* = 3.732 is exactly
   **2 + √3**, the signature of a degenerate-2×2 `escalc` input; *Gierszewski et
   al. 2018*'s *d* = 2.43 is inflated by the structural zero cell at first choice.
3. **Sign error.** *Howard et al. 1998*: Davies records **+1.14** for a study that
   found **no copying** (females retained their original preference 15:5); the
   re-extraction and Jones both give **−0.61**.
4. **Pool-vs-split, not an error.** *Fowler-Finn et al. 2015* is the one case where
   neither is wrong: Davies pooled one effect (*d* 0.63) where Jones — and our
   re-extraction — split it into a copying arm (*d* 1.21) and a no-copying arm
   (*d* 0.10), matching the study's significant phenotype × age interaction.

A design subtlety surfaced during re-extraction: for *Dugatkin 1992* the copying
tests are correctly compared against the study's **naïve control group**, not
against chance. Under that (correct) design Davies's total **N = 40** is right —
only the *magnitude* (the 2 + √3 artifact) is wrong — while Jones's third recorded
value for this study (lnOR 2.234) is spurious: it reproduces from no pair of the
paper's six experiments and should be dropped.

## How many *unique* primary studies are there? {#sec-unique-studies}

The PRISMA flowchart counts 40 studies carried over from Jones & DuVal
[-@jonesMechanismsSocialInfluence2019], 58 from Davies et al.
[-@daviesMetaanalysisFactorsInfluencing2020] and 29 from our own extraction.
Those three numbers do **not** sum to a count of unique primary studies, because
the sources overlap, which is worth making explicit.
This section resolves the corpus to distinct primary studies.

A **study** here is one primary publication contributing at least one usable
effect size. Three things have to be settled to count them:

1. **Matches across sources.** No two sources share an identifier, so candidates
   are generated from metadata and then adjudicated.
2. **Duplicates *within* a source.** One source can carry the same primary study
   under two identifiers.
3. **Records that look like matches but are not** — same author, adjacent year,
   same species, different paper.

### Candidate generation, then explicit adjudication

Candidates are generated deliberately generously: same first-author surname
prefix (three letters, accent-folded) and publication year within ±1. Species is
reported alongside but never used to *filter*, so no potential match is silently
discarded. Every candidate the data produce is then resolved by hand — accepted
or rejected, with the reason recorded — against the source records (authors,
year, species, sample size, outcome construct) and, where the two source teams
disagreed, against the primary PDFs already consulted for the
[re-extraction](#sec-reextraction) above.

The adjudication is an explicit list rather than a threshold rule, and the code
**fails loudly** if the data ever change such that a candidate is left
unresolved, or a hand-coded decision no longer corresponds to a real candidate.
That way the count cannot drift silently.

```{r}
tex_escape <- function(x) {
    x <- as.character(x)
    # Protect the \allowbreak{} break hints inserted by breakable() before the
    # backslash and brace escaping runs, then restore them afterwards.
    x <- gsub("\\allowbreak{}", "\u0001", x, fixed = TRUE)
    x <- gsub("\\", "\\textbackslash{}", x, fixed = TRUE)
    for (ch in c("&", "%", "$", "#", "_", "{", "}"))

    x <- gsub("\u0001", "\\allowbreak{}", x, fixed = TRUE)
```

### Two judgement calls worth flagging

Neither changes the count, but both are decisions rather than deductions.

* **Which Jones & DuVal 1992 code pairs with Davies' 1992 record.** Davies'
  study 27 is labelled "Dugatkin and Godin 1992" but carries the data of the
  single-authored Dugatkin (1992), which is `DUG1992SEX`; `DUG1992REV` is then
  Jones-only. Pairing them the other way round would swap which study is
  Jones-only without altering any total, since 1992 contributes two distinct
  publications either way.
* **Treating `GIE2018USI` as a within-Jones duplicate.** This follows the
  [re-extraction finding](#sec-reextraction) that the two codes carry the same
  two reversal-vs-control contrasts. Counting them as two studies instead would
  make the corpus `r n_unique + 1` studies rather than `r n_unique`.

## Summary of the cross-check

Four findings emerge from the comparison, and they are the basis for the
sensitivity analyses reported elsewhere in this supplement.

* **Re-extraction (the > 0.5 set).** Returning to the source PDFs for the
  `r nrow(reext)` effect sizes that disagreed most, the design-blind re-derivation
  reproduced the **point estimates** wherever the data are discrete counts (mean
  |Δ*d*| vs Jones = `r fmt(mj,2)`; vs Davies = `r fmt(md,2)`), **corrected** Jones
  on the single-group / vs-chance studies (a 4/n phantom-control variance inflation
  from the 50/50 rule, with halved *N*), and isolated **Davies** as the divergent
  extraction — via different outcome constructs, `escalc` artifacts, a sign error,
  and one defensible pool-vs-split. This is an independent re-derivation, **not** a
  validation against Jones: the point-estimate agreement is expected for discrete
  counts and the variance was no longer reverse-engineered to match.
* **Overlap.** Of `r nrow(shared) + nrow(unmatched)` Jones & DuVal studies,
  **`r n_shared` are shared** with Davies; `r nrow(unmatched)` are unique to
  Jones (`r sum(unmatched$n_effect_sizes)` effect sizes). Those counts key on
  (author prefix, year), which merges the two distinct Dugatkin 1998
  publications into one; the [study-level crosswalk](#sec-unique-studies)
  separates them and puts the shared count at `r n_jd`.
* **Unique primary studies.** Resolving all three sources to distinct
  publications gives **`r n_unique` unique primary studies**, not the
  `r n_naive` implied by summing the source counts: `r n_jd` studies sit in
  both source meta-analyses, `r n_dn` of our new studies were already in
  Davies et al., and one Jones & DuVal study code is an internal duplicate.
* **Agreement.** Across `r n_pairs` comparable effect-size pairs, Jones-converted
  and Davies magnitudes correlate at Pearson *r* = `r fmt(pear$estimate,2)`
  (`r fmt(100*n_match/n_pairs,0)`% within tolerance), rising to
  *r* = `r fmt(pear_sub$estimate,2)` among studies where both teams extracted
  the same number of effect sizes.
* **Direction of disagreement.** Mean Bland–Altman bias =
  `r fmt(bias,2)` *d*-units, indicating Davies reports
  `r if (bias < 0) "slightly larger" else "slightly smaller"` magnitudes on
  average; most large discrepancies trace to **different outcomes extracted**
  (behaviour-type columns) or **different effect-size partitioning**, not to the
  metric conversion.
