\subsection*{Small-study effects, time-lag patterns, and sensitivity analyses}

We found strong evidence of small-study effects, while mixed evidence of time-lag bias using the combined dataset (original + new primary studies). Funnel plots based on model residuals showed clear asymmetry for both types of effect size (\hyperref[fig:supplementaryfunnelhedges]{Supplementary Figure~\ref*{fig:supplementaryfunnelhedges}} and \hyperref[fig:supplementaryfunnelor]{Supplementary Figure~\ref*{fig:supplementaryfunnelor}}). The Egger's-type multilevel regression slopes of sampling variance were positive and statistically significant for both datasets (Odds ratio: $\beta_{\sqrt{v_i}}$ = 2.11, 95\% CI: 1.23 to 2.99, Fig.~6 of the main article; Hedges' \textit{g}: $\beta_{\sqrt{v_i}}$ = 4.84, 95\% CI: 3.59 to 6.09, Fig.~7 of the main article). For the odds ratio dataset, we found evidence that publication year was negatively associated with effect size magnitude ($\beta_{year}$ = -0.028, 95\% CI: -0.048 to -0.007; Fig.~6 of the main article). However, for the Hedges' \textit{g} dataset, we found little evidence that publication year was negatively associated with effect size magnitude ($\beta_{year}$ = -0.005, 95\% CI: -0.018 to 0.008;  Fig.~7 of the main article). 

The two correction frameworks we used gave divergent adjusted means and provided smaller estimates than the uncorrected overall estimates. On the Hedges' \textit{g} scale, the bias-corrected intercept~\cite{nakagawaMethodsTestingPublication2022} fell to $-0.21$ (95\% CI: $-0.48$ to $0.06$), a mean indistinguishable from zero, whereas the bias-robust estimator~\cite{yangRobustPointVariance2024} retained a positive but reduced mean of $0.34$ (95\% CI: 0.25 to 0.42). The odds ratio scale showed the same pattern: the bias-corrected mean approached the null at a log odds ratio of $0.10$ (95\% CI: $-0.23$ to 0.43; \textit{i.e.}, OR = 1.11), while the bias-robust mean remained clearly positive at a log odds ratio of $0.55$ (95\% CI: 0.39 to 0.71; OR = 1.73). Both corrected means are shown alongside the uncorrected estimate in Fig.~3 of the main article and Fig.~4 of the main article.

Leave-one-out analyses confirmed the robustness of both the overall odds ratio and Hedges' \textit{g} on the combined datasets. Sequential exclusion of individual studies yielded consistent pooled estimates, with every confidence interval overlapping the full-sample estimate and no single study exerting disproportionate influence. The largest shift in the pooled mean was only 0.02
Hedges' \textit{g} units for the continuous outcomes and 0.04 log-odds units for the binary outcomes (\hyperref[fig:supplementaryloostudyhedges]{Supplementary Figure~\ref*{fig:supplementaryloostudyhedges}} and \hyperref[fig:supplementaryloostudyor]{Supplementary Figure~\ref*{fig:supplementaryloostudyor}}). The results are also robust to the more rigorous leave-one-species-out analyses, which remove entire species, despite the uneven phylogenetic sampling, with all overall effect sizes remaining significantly positive (\hyperref[fig:supplementaryloospecieshedges]{Supplementary Figure~\ref*{fig:supplementaryloospecieshedges}} and \hyperref[fig:supplementaryloospeciesor]{Supplementary Figure~\ref*{fig:supplementaryloospeciesor}}). Here, the largest shift was 0.04 Hedges' \textit{g} units (when omitting \textit{Poecilia latipinna}) and 0.07 log-odds units. Removing \textit{Poecilia reticulata}---the most-studied and strongest-copying species, which alone contributes nearly a quarter of the continuous effect sizes---nudged the Hedges' \textit{g} mean toward zero, as expected, but did not change the sign or significance of either synthesis.

The combined estimates showed little sensitivity to the contentious effect sizes on both scales (Hedges' \textit{g} or odds ratio). The pooled Hedges' \textit{g} synthesis was essentially unchanged, the original 0.45 (95\% CI: 0.25 to  0.66, $k = 227$) became 0.41 (95\% CI: 0.22 to 0.60, $k = 213$) when the flagged studies were dropped, and 0.42 (95\% CI: 0.23 to 0.60, $k = 225$) when they were corrected, remaining significant throughout (\hyperref[fig:supplementarysensitivityhedges]{Supplementary
Figure~\ref*{fig:supplementarysensitivityhedges}}). 
The odds ratio synthesis was also stable: the pooled odds ratio of 1.81
(95\% CI: 1.36 to 2.42, $k = 172$) became 1.77 (95\% CI: 1.32 to 2.38, $k = 156$) under leave-out and 1.79 (95\% CI: 1.36 to 2.34, $k = 169$) under correction (\hyperref[fig:supplementarysensitivityor]{Supplementary
Figure~\ref*{fig:supplementarysensitivityor}}).

\begin{figure}[H]

\end{center}
\caption{\textbf{Funnel plots of Hedges' g data.} Both panels plot each effect size against its precision ($1/SE$), but differ in what is removed before plotting: (\textbf{a}) Marginal residuals are the deviations of each observed effect from the fixed-effect (intercept, the overall mean) prediction, so they still carry all of the random-effects heterogeneity (between-study, between-effect-size, and between-species--\textit{i.e.} the hierarchical non-independence of effect sizes). Asymmetry here (defined in Methods) is the classic visual signature of small-study and publication bias. (\textbf{b}) Conditional residuals additionally subtract the estimated random intercepts (study, effect-size, and species BLUPs), so the heterogeneity captured by the multilevel structure is partialled out. A symmetric, evenly-filled funnel here means that once the multilevel structure is accounted for, there is little precision-related asymmetry left, \textit{i.e.}, the asymmetry seen in (\textbf{a}) is largely explained by the modelled random effects rather than by selective reporting. Any asymmetry that persists in (\textbf{b}) is the more credible visual indication of true small-study effects.}
\label{fig:supplementaryfunnelhedges}
\end{figure}
